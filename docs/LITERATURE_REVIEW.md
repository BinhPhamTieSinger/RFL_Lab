# Heterogeneity, not attackers, breaks most FL defenses

*Literature review compiled 2026-11-14 for RFL Lab from 170 sources (classical work through November 2026). Statements about the team's own experiments refer to `docs/EXPERIMENT_LOG.md`.*

Almost every robust aggregation rule published since 2017 judges a client by comparing it with its peers, and non-IID data breaks that comparison before any attacker arrives: Krum, coordinate median and geometric median lose 10 to 70 accuracy points on skewed data with zero attackers, and theory proves a heterogeneity-dependent error floor that no aggregator removes ([ICLR 2022](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2006.09365v5); [NeurIPS 2023](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2309.13591)). The attacks a 2026 defense must withstand (ALIE, Min-Max/Min-Sum, mimic, PoisonedFL, A3FL, IBA, and the personalization-aware PFedBA and Bad-PFL) are built to sit inside the honest spread, and independent 2025 benchmarks find that self-reported defense results rarely hold up: under non-IID data there is "almost no robust defense", and trigger-optimising backdoors defeat every anomaly detector tested ([SoK FLPoison, preprint](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.03801); [BackFed, preprint](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.04903)). What holds up best is either influence bounding with worst-case guarantees (worker momentum, nearest-neighbour mixing, trimmed mean, adaptive clipping) or trust anchored outside cross-client geometry (FLTrust's root data, per-client reconstruction, planted indicator tasks, output-space scores on server data such as the Alharbi group's RKD and SD-CSFL). The Alharbi line is three papers, not four: RFCL is the method of the ECAI 2023 paper, and none of the three tests CIFAR-100, partial participation or a hierarchy. Personalized, clustered and hierarchical FL each shift robustness without securing it: a local head blunts black-box backdoors but not pFL-aware ones, and every hierarchical defense found acts level by level, trusts its middle tier and assumes fixed groups. Privacy-compatible screening exists only for simple predicates such as norm bounds and cosine to a public reference, or for group aggregates as in SHARE. For a paper built on FedHEP, the open ground is specific: in-distribution attackers that no outlier screen catches (the team's ALIE and Min-Max result), false isolation of honest clients as disagreement grows (unmeasured anywhere in the literature), backdoor defense for personalized hierarchies without a clean-accuracy cost, and hierarchy-native mechanisms (fused parent and Global evidence, discounting of compromised parents, attacker-placement threat models, head-disagreement signals) evaluated to a standard that most published defenses do not meet.

## Non-IID data breaks distance-based aggregators before any attacker arrives

### Classical rules assume honest updates cluster, and label skew removes that assumption

The first generation of Byzantine-robust aggregation replaced the mean with a robust statistic. **Krum** (Blanchard et al., NeurIPS 2017) scores each update by the summed squared distance to its n − f − 2 nearest neighbours and outputs the lowest-scoring one (Multi-Krum averages the m best); it needs 2f + 2 < n, knowledge of f, costs O(n²d), and the same paper proves that any linear rule can be steered anywhere by a single Byzantine worker ([Krum, NeurIPS 2017](https://papers.NeurIPS.cc/paper_files/paper/2017/file/f4b9ec30ad9f68f89b29639786cb62ef-Paper.pdf)). **Coordinate-wise median and trimmed mean** (Yin et al., ICML 2018) give order-optimal error rates for strongly convex losses at O(nd) and O(nd log n) cost ([Yin et al., ICML 2018](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v80/yin18a.html)). **Bulyan** (El Mhamdi et al., ICML 2018) argues that earlier rules leave the attacker a per-round margin growing with dimension and claims to cut it to O(1/√d) ([Bulyan, ICML 2018](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v80/mhamdi18a.html)); its commonly quoted requirement n ≥ 4f + 3 is recalled rather than verified in the notes, and if correct it excludes attacker fractions of 25% and above. **RFA** (Pillutla et al., arXiv 2019; IEEE Trans. Signal Processing 2022) aggregates with the geometric median using a few calls to an ordinary averaging oracle, which makes it compatible with secure aggregation and agnostic to f ([RFA](https://arxiv.org/abs/1912.13445v2)). **Norm clipping with weak DP** (Sun et al., arXiv 2019) rescales each update to a norm bound M; a bound of 3 neutralised an EMNIST backdoor with almost no main-task loss ([Sun et al.](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1911.07963)).

All of these judge a client against its peers, and that is exactly what non-IID data undermines. With **zero attackers**, moving MNIST from IID to non-IID drops Krum from 97.95% to 89.90%, coordinate median from 97.72% to 80.36% and RFA from 98.62% to 82.60%, because "no single worker is representative" when local data differ ([Karimireddy, He, Jaggi, ICLR 2022](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2006.09365v5)). On non-IID CIFAR-10 with FedSGD, median and geometric median fall to near random guessing (about 10%) with no attack while mean and trimmed mean stay around 80% ([Li, Ngai, Voigt, IEEE Trans. Big Data (accepted)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2302.07173)). The **mimic attack**, in which every attacker copies one real honest client, cannot be filtered at all yet still cuts Krum to 37.33%, median to 64.27% and RFA to 78.93% on non-IID MNIST, while centered clipping holds 91.53% and plain averaging 92.73% ([ICLR 2022](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2006.09365v5)). Selection-type rules discard honest minority distributions; averaging-type rules let attackers through high dimensions, a two-sided diagnosis made explicitly by **GAS** (Liu et al., ICML 2023), whose coordinate-splitting fix yields only mild gains ([GAS](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2302.06079)).

### Theory sets a heterogeneity floor that no aggregator removes

The optimisation-theory line turns this into hard limits. For any algorithm, Byzantine error is at least **Ω(δζ²)**, where δ is the attacker fraction and ζ² bounds honest gradient dissimilarity ([ICLR 2022](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2006.09365v5)); the AISTATS 2023 paper states the same floor as **Ω((f/n)G²)** ([Allouah et al., AISTATS 2023](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v206/allouah23a.html)). When dissimilarity grows with the gradient norm, the (G, B) model gives a **breakdown point of 1/(2 + B²)**, strictly below one half, and a matched lower bound of order (f/(n − (2 + B²)f))·G²; the authors call plain bounded dissimilarity "arguably unrealistic" because it fails even for least squares ([Allouah et al., NeurIPS 2023](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2309.13591)). More local steps shrink the noise term but not the heterogeneity term, and sampling too few clients produces rounds with a Byzantine majority ([Allouah et al., ICML 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.12780v2)). Time matters too: any permutation-invariant rule that ignores history "can be made to eventually diverge" ([Karimireddy, He, Jaggi, ICML 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2012.10333)).

Three pre-aggregation steps have both proofs and independent support. **Worker momentum** makes honest momentums converge toward one another; momentum 0.99 matched the attack-free benchmark in RESAM (Farhadkhani et al., ICML 2022), whose analysis nonetheless assumes identically distributed honest workers ([RESAM](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.12173v1)). **Nearest-neighbour mixing (NNM)** replaces each input by the average of its n − f nearest neighbours and lifts any rule with constant robustness coefficient to the optimal O(f/n); on Dirichlet-0.1 MNIST with 4 of 17 attackers, worst-case accuracy rose from 10.19% (Krum) and 27.45% (trimmed mean) to **70.07% and 79.04%**, far above bucketing's 36.10% and 42.80% ([AISTATS 2023](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v206/allouah23a.html)). **Adaptive robust clipping (ARC)** clips the largest ⌊2(f/n)(n − f)⌋ inputs to the next norm; static clipping is provably not robust, whereas ARC (combined with NNM) raised worst-case CIFAR-10 accuracy at Dirichlet 0.075 from 13.7% to 62.7% for coordinate median and from 40.7% to 60.5% for trimmed mean ([Allouah et al., ICLR 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.14432v5)). Bucketing (ICLR 2022) helps but its randomness "highly compromises the worst-case robustness" ([AISTATS 2023](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v206/allouah23a.html)). For personalized FL, the NeurIPS 2024 analysis shows the optimal collaboration weight shrinks as heterogeneity or attacker fraction grows, which suggests graded down-weighting rather than binary exclusion ([Allouah et al., NeurIPS 2024](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2409.20329)).

### Similarity, trust and detection rules each hide a knob that carries non-IID sensitivity

**FoolsGold** (Fung et al., RAID 2020) down-weights clients whose accumulated histories are unusually similar, assuming sybils resemble each other; a single attacker defeats it, as its authors note ([FoolsGold](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1808.04866.pdf)). **FLTrust** (Cao et al., NDSS 2021) anchors trust to a server update computed on 100 clean examples, weighting each client by ReLU(cosine) and rescaling to the server norm; it claims robustness at 40-60% attackers, but a fully biased root set pushes CIFAR-10 error to 0.90 ([FLTrust](https://arxiv.org/abs/2012.13995)). **AFA** (Muñoz-González et al., arXiv 2019, preprint) scores cosine similarity to the aggregate, flags clients beyond median ∓ ξσ with ξ growing 0.5 per pass, and permanently blocks clients through a Beta-posterior reputation; the authors warn small priors "may also block benign ones" ([AFA](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1909.05125)). **DnC** (Shejwalkar and Houmansadr, NDSS 2021) removes updates with large projections on the top singular vector of sub-sampled updates; its authors call non-IID "a limitation of DnC in its current form" after Min-Sum cut it from 86.6% to 7.3% on FEMNIST ([NDSS 2021](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10286354)). **FLAME** (Nguyen et al., USENIX Security 2022) combines HDBSCAN on cosine distances, median-norm clipping and adaptive noise ([FLAME](https://arxiv.org/abs/2101.02281)); **SignGuard** (Xu et al., ICDCS 2022) filters on norms and sign statistics and found that adding cosine features "can hurt" ([SignGuard](https://arxiv.org/abs/2109.05872v2)); **FreqFed** (Fereidooni et al., NDSS 2024) clusters low-frequency DCT coefficients ([FreqFed](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2312.04432)); **MESAS** (Krauß and Dmitrienko, ACM CCS 2023) cascades six per-layer metrics through statistical tests, cutting random-non-IID backdoor accuracy from 77.37% to 2.37% at 24.37 s overhead ([MESAS](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.03600v2)).

The 2022-2026 detectors move away from raw cross-client geometry. **FLDetector** (Zhang et al., KDD 2022) predicts each update from its history with an L-BFGS Hessian estimate and removes inconsistent clients ([FLDetector](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.09209v3)); **FedREDefense** (Xie, Fang, Gong, ICML 2024) tests each update in isolation by reconstruction error and assumes neither similar distributions nor a benign majority ([FedREDefense](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v235/xie24c.html)); **LASA** (Xu, Zhang, Hu, WACV 2025) and **AlignIns** (Xu, Zhang, Hu, CVPR 2025) apply median-centred z-scores per layer or to cosine-with-global-model plus top-k sign agreement, AlignIns reaching 80.48% robust accuracy on non-IID CIFAR-10 versus 39.36% for RFA and 37.78% for Multi-Krum at 0.13 s per round ([AlignIns](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.07978v2); [LASA](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.01435v1)). **FoundationFL** (Fang et al., NDSS 2025) argues new rules are unnecessary: inject synthetic copies of a selected update and run plain trimmed mean or median, robust to 45% attackers in its own tests ([FoundationFL](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.17381v1)). **FedDLAD** (Ding et al., IJCAI 2025) adds an IQR sign-flip step and a "pardon" that re-admits untrusted updates, gaining about 1.3 points ([FedDLAD](https://www.ijcai.org/proceedings/2025/0559.pdf)). **Learnable aggregation weights** (Parsa et al., ICLR 2026) optimise client weights jointly with the model and report the largest gains at high heterogeneity ([ICLR 2026](https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2026/hash/43a1c817602662be3a59015e9a86d0d1-Abstract-Conference.html)). Several 2025-2026 preprints (GeminiGuard, H+, FedGreed, EnCAgg, FedAOT, GShield) extend these ideas without peer review ([GShield, preprint](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.19286); [EnCAgg, preprint](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.22506)).

Defenses avoid needing f in five ways: majority clustering, a test for whether a round is bimodal at all (FLDetector's gap statistic, MESAS's significance tests), a fixed z-score radius (AlignIns, LASA, AFA), an absolute anchor (FLTrust, BackdoorIndicator, FedREDefense), or soft threshold-free weighting (FoolsGold; the Fed-Credit preprint). Each hides a hyperparameter: AlignIns admits "some benign updates may be erroneously discarded" and that the best radii vary by dataset and attack ([AlignIns](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.07978v2)). **Hard permanent exclusion is rare**, and where it appears the costs are documented: FLDetector's 40% precision "removes many benign clients and can harm the global model" ([BackFed, preprint 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.04903v2)), and the FedCAP personalized-FL preprint's fixed norm threshold misses 100% of ALIE attackers on CIFAR-10 ([FedCAP, preprint](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.13083)). FedRecover (Cao et al.; arXiv 2022, commonly cited as IEEE S&P 2023, venue unconfirmed in the notes) rebuilds a clean model from stored history after removal but degrades when the detector's false-negative rate reaches 0.4 ([FedRecover](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2210.10936)).

### Which rules still stand in 2025-2026

Independent benchmarks are sobering. The **FLPoison SoK** (Zhang et al., preprint 2025; 17 defenses, 15 attacks, 2,040 settings) ranks median, FLTrust, DnC and FLAME best against model poisoning, finds all defenses worse under non-IID and only FLTrust near 80% there, and measures costs from 15-53x the plain mean for robust statistics to 1,634x for FoolsGold and about 1,710,000x for DeepSight ([SoK FLPoison](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.03801)). What survives best is either trust-anchored (FLTrust, with its own breaks below) or the composition of **momentum, NNM, trimmed mean and ARC**, whose guarantees are worst-case rather than attack-specific. One realism caveat frames everything: in production cross-device FL, compromised fractions are at most about 0.1% for data poisoning and 0.01% for model poisoning, and there norm bounding is "as robust as the costlier AGRs" ([Shejwalkar et al., IEEE S&P 2022](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2108.10241)). Attacker fractions of 20-40% are a deliberate stress test and must be argued as such.

## A 2026 defense must survive attacks that hide inside the honest spread

### Untargeted attacks moved from crude noise to statistics-matching and knowledge-free history attacks

The canonical untargeted attacks share one idea: stay statistically close enough to honest updates that robust statistics cannot separate them. **ALIE, "A Little Is Enough"** (Baruch, Baruch, Goldberg, NeurIPS 2019) sends μ + z·σ per coordinate, with z chosen so that the attackers attract enough honest workers; at 20% corrupted workers it cut CIFAR-10 accuracy by 66% under Krum, 52% under Bulyan and 45% under trimmed mean, and needs no benign updates under IID data ([ALIE](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1902.06156.pdf)). Two z formulas circulate (the paper's worked example gives z = 1.75 for n = 50, m = 24, while the ICLR 2022 benchmark uses z ≈ 0.25 for n = 25, m = 5), so the z used should be stated and swept ([ICLR 2022](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2006.09365)). **IPM** (Xie, Koyejo, Gupta, UAI 2019) sends the negated, ε-scaled benign mean ([IPM, UAI 2019](https://mlanthology.org/uai/2019/xie2019uai-fall/)). The **Fang attack** (Fang et al., USENIX Security 2020) maximises directed deviation against a known rule, for example placing values just beyond the benign extremes for trimmed mean, raising MNIST Krum error from 0.11 to 0.75 with only partial knowledge ([Fang et al.](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1911.11815)). **Min-Max and Min-Sum** (Shejwalkar and Houmansadr, NDSS 2021) take benign reference + γ·perturbation and binary-search the largest γ whose distances stay within the benign range; they cause 1.5x to 60x more accuracy loss than prior attacks ([NDSS 2021](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10286354)). The **mimic attack** (ICLR 2022) copies an honest client and is purpose-built for non-IID data ([ICLR 2022](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2006.09365)).

The 2022-2026 additions need neither benign updates nor the aggregation rule. **MPAF** (Cao and Gong, CVPR Workshops 2022 per the notes, venue unconfirmed) has fake clients push toward a random base model with a large scale factor and drives FedAvg to random guessing with 1% fake clients ([MPAF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2203.08669)). **PoisonedFL** (Xie, Fang, Gong, CVPR 2025) keeps a fixed random sign vector across rounds so malicious updates stop cancelling, and self-calibrates its magnitude from a binomial test on the global-model history; it breaks Multi-Krum, median, trimmed mean, norm bound, FLTrust, FLAME, FLCert and FLDetector, reaching random guessing or worse in 29 of 45 cases, and FLDetector marks its clients benign precisely because they are consistent ([PoisonedFL](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.15611v3)). **XFED** (Mouri et al., arXiv April 2026, preprint) is non-collusive: each attacker perturbs its own benign update by a magnitude derived from the median and MAD of recent global deltas, and reportedly bypasses eight defenses including FLAME, FreqFed and SignGuard, with FLTrust the stated exception ([XFED, preprint](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.09489)). Other recent work includes a threshold-aware optimisation framework that breaks FLAME, FLDetector and centered clipping (Yang et al., arXiv 2024, venue unconfirmed) ([Yang et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.15267v1)), "poisoning with a pill", which hides poison in about one neuron per layer then matches benign cosine and distance (Guo et al., arXiv 2024; the PDF carries an AAAI 2026 notice, otherwise unconfirmed) ([pill](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.15389)), and the hybrid sparse attack HSA (Ozfatura et al., preprint, v2 March 2026) ([HSA, preprint](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.06230v2)).

**Heterogeneity strengthens the stealthy attacks.** The FLPoison SoK finds "MinMax and MinSum attacks are less effective under IID but perform better under non-IID settings" and that non-IID leaves "almost no robust defense" ([SoK FLPoison](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.03801)); a NeurIPS 2025 WiML poster reports that ALIE and IPM calibrated for non-IID variance often outperform Min-Max and Min-Sum, though only the abstract was available ([Errami et al.](https://neurips.cc/virtual/2025/133961)). Data poisoning is weaker: a targeted label flip at 40% attackers drives CIFAR-10 source-class recall to 0% while global accuracy moves only from 78.3% to 74.4% ([Tolpegin et al.](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/2007.08432)), so per-class metrics are required; and under label poisoning with sufficiently heterogeneous data, the plain mean is provably more robust than robust aggregators ([Peng et al., arXiv 2404.13647](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2404.13647)).

### Backdoors are now judged on stealth, durability and trigger optimisation

Backdoor evaluation has split into three properties. **Durability** attacks write into rarely updated coordinates: Neurotoxin (Zhang et al., ICML 2022) projects the poison off the top-k benign coordinates and roughly doubles backdoor lifespan ([Neurotoxin](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2206.10341)); Chameleon (Dai and Li, ICML 2023) uses contrastive learning and lasts 661 rounds versus 112 for Neurotoxin on a CIFAR-100 semantic backdoor ([Chameleon](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2304.12961)). **Trigger optimisation**: A3FL (Zhang et al., NeurIPS 2023) optimises the trigger against a simulated unlearning global model and reaches 99.75% ASR under norm clipping with one compromised client versus 13.9% for the best baseline, defeating all 12 defenses it tested ([A3FL](https://faculty.ist.psu.edu/wu/papers/A3FL-NeurIPS_2023.pdf)); IBA (Nguyen et al., NeurIPS 2023) learns an imperceptible trigger generator and keeps about 95% of peak backdoor accuracy 250 rounds after the attack stops ([IBA](https://papers.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2023/file/d0c6bc641a56bebee9d985b937307367-Paper-Conference.pdf)); F3BA (Fang and Chen, AAAI 2023) flips the signs of the least important 1% of parameters and fully evades Bulyan at 8 of 20 attackers ([F3BA](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2301.08170)). **Explicit evasion**: Edge-case backdoors (Wang et al., NeurIPS 2020) use tail inputs and prove backdoor detection NP-hard in the worst case ([Wang et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2007.05084)); DarkFed (Li et al., IJCAI 2024) is data-free and lowers similarity among colluders to defeat clustering, with 98.51% ASR under FLAME ([DarkFed](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.03299v1)); the layer-wise LP attack (Zhuang et al., ICLR 2024) poisons only backdoor-critical layers ([LP attack](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.04466)), and its successor LSA (Foroughi et al., arXiv 2026, stated accepted to IEEE ICC 2026) reaches 96.98% under FLAME ([LSA](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.15161)). 3DFed (IEEE S&P 2023) adds decoys and an in-model acceptance indicator ([3DFed code](https://github.com/haoyangliASTAPLE/3DFed)), but its strength is contested: the FLAME authors dispute it and BackFed found a bug in its DeepSight code ([BackFed](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.04903)). The suite of a recent top-venue backdoor defense, MARS (Wan et al., NeurIPS 2025), is a good benchmark: model replacement, CerP, 3DFed and a custom adaptive attack in the main text, with A3FL, Chameleon, semantic and partial-layer attacks in the appendix, at Dirichlet α ∈ {0.2, 0.5, 0.9} ([MARS](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.20383)).

### Personalized, clustered and hierarchical FL have their own attack literature

Personalization does not remove the threat; it changes where a backdoor must survive. Partial model sharing (FedRep, FedBN) "can significantly boost robustness against backdoor attacks", pushing FedRep ASR below 10% (Qin et al., KDD 2023) ([Qin et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.01677)). Attacks then target that robustness. **BapFL** (Ye et al., ACM journal, name unconfirmed) poisons only the shared encoder and diversifies the classifier ([BapFL](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.15971)); **PFedBA** (Lyu et al., USENIX Security 2024) aligns backdoor and main-task gradients so the backdoor survives clean personalization, exceeding 80% ASR on all 10 pFL algorithms tested and at least 87% under Multi-Krum, trimmed mean, DnC and FLAME for most of them on Fashion-MNIST ([PFedBA](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.06207v1)); **Bad-PFL** (Fan et al., ICLR 2025) uses triggers built from natural target-class features, which benign personalization reinforces, reaching 97.95% ASR on FedRep and 77.21% under median ([Bad-PFL](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.12736v1)). For clustered FL, Vo et al. (IEEE S&P 2025) poison the clustering step itself, inducing dissimilar clients to share a cluster, and beat FLTrust and FLAME with up to 48% accuracy loss ([Vo et al., S&P 2025](https://research-repository.griffith.edu.au/items/7955ed1f-56b6-431a-9e67-5f6619495648)). Hierarchical FL has only an empirical assessment (Alqattan et al., arXiv 2024, venue unstated): untargeted attacks had small effects, but backdoor success rose with the malicious fraction and the 4-level hierarchy was the most vulnerable ([Alqattan et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.10752v1)). **No dedicated strong attack on hierarchical personalized FL was found**, which leaves the paper free, and obliged, to define its own structure-aware adversary.

### Adaptive attacks follow a small number of accepted templates

Reviewers expect a defense-aware attacker built by one of a few published recipes. The Shejwalkar-Houmansadr template replaces the Min-Max distance bound with the defense's own acceptance test and binary-searches the largest γ that passes; the authors used it on their own DnC defense ([NDSS 2021](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10286354)). FLTrust's adaptive attack substitutes the rule into the directed-deviation objective and solves it by zeroth-order projected gradient ascent ([FLTrust](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2012.13995)). MARS adds λ times the defense's own score to the attacker's loss and sweeps λ; at λ ≥ 0.05 their original rule failed (TPR 0%) and they had to redesign it ([MARS](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.20383)). PoisonedFL shows that even a defense-agnostic attacker can self-calibrate from global-model history. Temporal attacks on reputation are documented as **on-off** and good-mouthing/bad-mouthing attacks (Lewis et al., JMLR 2023) ([Lewis et al.](https://www.jmlr.org/papers/v24/22-0014.html)). The realism counterweight remains IEEE S&P 2022: 20-40% attackers need a cross-silo or worst-case justification, and the paper recommends evaluating with strong adaptive attacks and practical parameter ranges ([Shejwalkar et al., S&P 2022](https://people.cs.umass.edu/~amir/papers/2022-SP-poisoning-FL.pdf)).

## Backdoor defenses that leave weight-space cosine hold up best

### Independent benchmarks contradict most self-reported backdoor results

Two 2025 benchmarks reset expectations. **BackFed** (Dao et al., preprint 2025, also listed on the ICLR 2026 site; CIFAR-10 ResNet-18, 100 clients, 10 per round, Dirichlet 0.5, 10% attackers) finds that "dynamic attacks (A3FL and IBA) defeat all anomaly detection defenses": FedAvg, median, RFA, trimmed mean, FoolsGold and norm clipping all leave **90-100% ASR** under A3FL and IBA, with RLR (64.5% and 60.8%) and Krum (31.9% on A3FL) the only partial exceptions ([BackFed](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.04903); [ICLR 2026 listing](https://iclr.cc/virtual/2026/10019346)). Detection precision is poor: FLAME 4.1% precision and 11.5% recall on a fixed-pattern backdoor, DeepSight 6.7/10.4, FLDetector about 40/20; false-positive rates in attack-free rounds reach 39.3% for FLAME, 25.6% for DeepSight and 99.1% for BackdoorIndicator. The gap between self-reported and third-party numbers is the central lesson: FLAME reports 0% backdoor accuracy in its own paper but 84.2% ASR in BackFed; AlignIns reports 1.77% but shows 60.67% in the FedSurrogate preprint's table ([FLAME](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2101.02281); [FedSurrogate, preprint](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.11122)). The FLPoison SoK reaches the same verdict for non-IID data ([SoK](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.03801)).

The mechanism behind these failures is consistent. With a small poisoned learning rate and Dirichlet 0.2 data, FLAME's TPR fell to 0 while backdoor accuracy stayed above 80%, because "a poisoned update can lie closer to a benign update than other benign updates do" ([BackdoorIndicator](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.20862v1)); pairwise cosine "only captures their general directional similarity" and misses sign-level differences ([AlignIns](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.07978v2)). A backdoor trained at normal learning rate barely moves whole-model direction, so a cosine screen that catches sign flips easily is close to blind to it.

### Defenses with the strongest reported evidence measure something other than global cosine

The defenses that report holding up use signals that do not depend on cross-client weight similarity. **Sign and coordinate agreement**: RLR (Ozdayi et al., AAAI 2021) sets per-coordinate learning rates from sign agreement at 0.02 s per round, but costs heavy accuracy on FEMNIST (27.9% vs 74.8%) ([RLR](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.03767); [BackFed](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.04903)); AlignIns adds top-k sign agreement and reports CIFAR-10 ResNet-9 and CIFAR-100 results, the closest published match to a ResNet-9 CIFAR-100 setting ([AlignIns](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.07978v2)). **Subspace isolation**: Lockdown (Huang et al., NeurIPS 2023) trains each client in a sparse mask and keeps only consensus parameters, cutting ASR from 86.4% to 3.4% on non-IID CIFAR-10 ResNet-9 at 0.25x communication, though its threshold depends on the unknown attacker count and an "Omniscience" adaptive attack succeeds ([Lockdown](https://par.nsf.gov//servlets/purl/10515961)). **Planted tasks**: BackdoorIndicator (Li and Dai, USENIX Security 2024) plants an out-of-distribution indicator task and rejects uploads that preserve it; its own adaptive attack lowers TPR to 53.0%, recovered to 75.3% when combined with norm clipping ([BackdoorIndicator](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.20862v1)). **Per-client self-consistency**: FedREDefense (ICML 2024) reconstructs each update from about 10 distilled synthetic samples and flags reconstruction error above 0.6, reporting 100% detection and zero false positives down to Dirichlet 0.05 and up to 72% attackers, but it cannot detect updates produced by genuine training on poisoned data ([FedREDefense](https://par.nsf.gov//servlets/purl/10522911)). **Temporal features**: FLTracer (Zhang et al., IEEE TIFS 2024) runs a per-client Kalman filter over sign, ordering and classifier features, reporting 99.13% TPR and 2.29% FPR at Dirichlet 0.5 ([FLTracer](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.13424v1)). **Client-side validation**: CrowdGuard (Rieger et al., NDSS 2024) has clients test each other's hidden-layer behaviour inside TEEs, reporting 100% TPR/TNR at 45% malicious ([CrowdGuard](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2210.07714)). **Layer-restricted clustering with repair**: FedSurrogate (Abacha et al., arXiv May 2026, preprint) clusters only the five most dispersed layers and replaces flagged clients' critical layers rather than dropping them, with 2.3-9.1% FPR and 0.24% ASR on CIFAR-100, but breaks above about 35% attackers ([FedSurrogate, preprint](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.11122)). None of these has been tested against A3FL or IBA in an independent benchmark.

### The Alharbi line is three papers, and RFCL is the 2023 ECAI method

The four references named in the project brief are **three papers** from one Lancaster group: "RFCL" is the method name of the 2023 paper, whose exact title is "Robust Federated Learning Method against Data and Model Poisoning Attacks with Heterogeneous Data Distribution" (Alharbi, Marcolino, Gouglidis, Ni, ECAI 2023); no separate RFCL paper exists ([RFCL, ECAI 2023](https://eprints.lancs.ac.uk/204514/1/1129Alharbi.pdf); [dblp](https://dblp.uni-trier.de/rec/conf/ecai/AlharbiMGN23.html)). All three use 30 clients with full participation, and none tests CIFAR-100, ResNet-9 or a hierarchical multi-head model.

**RFCL** flattens weights, reduces them with PCA, clusters with HDBSCAN (outliers excluded), computes each cluster centre with a modified AFA rule that zero-weights flagged members **for that round only**, selects clusters by pairwise cosine between centres, and sends the aggregate only to selected clusters while others keep their own centre ("selective personalization"). It needs no server data, was tested at 10-60% attackers with Dirichlet 0.5 and 0.1 on MNIST, Fashion-MNIST and CIFAR-10 against IPM, ALIE, sign flipping, noise and label flipping, reports results only as figures, and **does not evaluate backdoors**; a later paper measured it at 2.75 s per round ([RFCL](https://eprints.lancs.ac.uk/204514/1/1129Alharbi.pdf); [SD-CSFL timing](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/WACV2026/papers/Alharbi_SD-CSFL_A_Synthetic_Data-Driven_Conformity_Scoring_Framework_for_Robust_Federated_WACV_2026_paper.pdf)). Structurally it is the closest published method to a parent-grouped hierarchy.

**RKD** (Alharbi, Marcolino, Ni, Gouglidis, IEEE SaTML 2025) clusters scalar cosine-to-global scores with HDBSCAN, treats the highest-mean cluster as benign, selects teachers within mean + k·σ of the coordinate-median L1 distance, and distils their averaged logits on an unlabeled server set equal to 16% of training data, with SWA producing the next global model. Against A3FL, F3BA, DBA, an anti-distillation backdoor and train-and-scale, it claims accuracy above 80% with ASR below 17%; ablations show filtering is needed at high attacker ratios and that removing distillation costs about 20 points of accuracy. It costs 42.03 s per round, and flagged clients can rejoin once re-classified ([RKD](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.00587v2)).

**SD-CSFL** (Alharbi, Kerim, Marcolino, Ni, WACV 2026) scores each client model by mean predictive entropy on a class-balanced Stable Diffusion calibration set (14,523 images for CIFAR-10) and keeps clients between the δ/2 and 1 − δ/2 percentiles of that round's scores, claiming a per-round false-positive bound of δ under approximate exchangeability. At 40% attackers on CIFAR-10, the best-F1 band gives 17% FP, 100% TP, F1 0.89 at α = 0.9 and 20/100/0.87 at α = 0.5, but the wider band's TP falls from 75% to 56% as α drops from 0.9 to 0.5; real calibration data slightly beats synthetic; cost is 19.45 s per round ([SD-CSFL, WACV 2026](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/WACV2026/papers/Alharbi_SD-CSFL_A_Synthetic_Data-Driven_Conformity_Scoring_Framework_for_Robust_Federated_WACV_2026_paper.pdf)). Because the band is a rank rule, a fixed share of clients is excluded every round whether or not an attack is present, so the δ bound is nearly true by construction and says little about how many attackers fall inside the band. No independent reproduction of any of the three papers was found.

### Distillation changes the attack surface without removing it

Aggregating predictions instead of weights is markedly more robust to untargeted Byzantine behaviour: because predictions live on a bounded simplex, prediction sharing on public data stayed accurate at 45% Byzantine clients where FedAvg collapsed at 10% (Roux, Zimmer, Pokutta, ICLR 2025), though the same paper builds two attacks that break per-sample robust aggregation and needs a history-aware defense to counter them ([Roux et al., ICLR 2025](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2402.12265)). Cronus (Chang et al., listed at NeurIPS 2021) shares soft labels, aggregates them with a robust mean estimator, and keeps 0.97-0.99 of its accuracy under the strongest attack ([Cronus](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1912.11279)). But plain logit averaging is not a backdoor defense: FedDF (Lin et al., NeurIPS 2020) initialises the student from the weight average, and A3FL defeats both FedDF and FedRAD (Sturluson et al., arXiv 2021, preprint) ([FedDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2006.07242); [A3FL](https://faculty.ist.psu.edu/wu/papers/A3FL-NeurIPS_2023.pdf)). Data-free variants such as FedGen (ICML 2021), FedFTG and DENSE were designed for heterogeneity and **have never been evaluated with malicious clients** ([FedGen](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2105.10056.pdf)). Measured server time per round ranges from 0.020 s (RLR) through 42.03 s (RKD) to 141.71 s (FedDF) and 198.77 s (FedBE) ([RKD](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.00587v2)).

### Synthetic probes and recovery fill two remaining roles

Server-side synthetic data serves as a fixed probe set (SD-CSFL), a generator trained against the global model (a cGAN preprint by Zafar et al., arXiv 2025, which collapses to 0.10 accuracy on Fashion-MNIST at α = 0.1 with no attack) ([Zafar et al., preprint](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.20884v3)), per-client inversion (FedInv, AAAI 2022; FedREDefense) ([FedInv](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AAAI/article/view/20903)), or flow-matching feature probes scored with a median + 3 × 1.4826 × MAD rule (FedFG, arXiv March 2026, preprint) ([FedFG, preprint](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.27986)). Any score that ranks clients by accuracy or entropy on class-balanced synthetic data penalises clients whose local data cover few classes, which is exactly what low Dirichlet α produces. After training, federated pruning reduces DBA ASR by over 70% at under 2% accuracy loss (Wu et al., arXiv 2020, preprint) ([Wu et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2011.01767)), but Lockdown reports that pruning suffers "poison-coupling" in FL; FedEraser (IEEE IWQoS 2021) and FedRecover replay stored history without the removed clients ([FedEraser](https://arxiv.org/abs/2012.13891v2)), and SCRUB-FL (arXiv June 2026, preprint) unlearns on synthesised trigger-like samples, reporting ASR as low as 3.88% ([SCRUB-FL, preprint](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.22700)). Detection without recovery leaves durable backdoors in place for hundreds of rounds.

## Personalization, grouping and peer review each shift robustness without securing it

### A local head blunts black-box backdoors but not pFL-aware ones

The personalized-FL evidence splits by threat model. **Ditto** (Li, Hu, Beirami, Smith, ICML 2021) regularises a personal model toward the global one and improved benign-device accuracy by about 6 points over the strongest robust baseline under label poisoning, Gaussian updates and model replacement; on FEMNIST with 80% label-poisoning devices it reached 0.669 versus 0.574 for the global model, but its theory is a toy linear setting and backdoors were not tested ([Ditto](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2012.04221)). Qin et al. (KDD 2023) explain why partial sharing resists black-box backdoors: the shared extractor still learns backdoor features, but the local linear classifier blocks the mapping to the target label; full-sharing methods such as Ditto and FedEM gained no robustness ([Qin et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.01677)). Then the pFL-aware attacks arrived: **BapFL** poisons only the encoder and noises the classifier, calling partial-sharing robustness a "false sense of security", and still reached 70.60% against Multi-Krum when combined with PGD ([BapFL](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2307.15971)); **Bad-PFL** reaches 94.12% ASR on Ditto and about 97% on FedRep even after 45 fine-tuning steps, with one compromised client sufficient for about 94% ([Bad-PFL](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2501.12736)). By 2025 the published consensus is that a never-uploaded head alone does not protect against a backdoor carried in the shared feature extractor. **FedCAP** (Li et al., arXiv 2024, venue unconfirmed) is the closest robust-pFL precedent for permanent isolation: per-client customised aggregation by softmax-scaled cosine, permanent removal above a fixed norm threshold, near-zero FPR, and a **100% false-negative rate against ALIE** on CIFAR-10, the failure mode of a detector that a stealthy attacker never trips ([FedCAP](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.13083v1)).

### Clustered and hierarchical defenses act level by level and trust the middle tier

Clustered defenses avoid branding honest minorities as attackers in three ways: give each cluster its own robustly aggregated model, as Byzantine-robust IFCA does with no detection at all, at the cost of needing the Byzantine fraction small relative to the **smallest** cluster (Tao, Yang, Kulkarni, arXiv 2023, preprint under review) ([Tao et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.00638)); cluster on signals less tied to label distribution, such as DFL-Dual's combination of model distance and Wasserstein distance between inverted dummy datasets (Sun et al., CVPR 2024), whose ablation shows each single-domain variant failing on at least one attack ([DFL-Dual](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/CVPR2024/papers/Sun_Byzantine-robust_Decentralized_Federated_Learning_via_Dual-domain_Clustering_and_Trust_Bootstrapping_CVPR_2024_paper.pdf)); or flag the smaller cluster, as FLAegis does (Marmol Campos et al., arXiv 2025, venue unstated), with mimic detection falling from about 0.94 to 0.8 ([FLAegis](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.18737)). That last heuristic is precisely the rule that mislabels an honest minority-distribution group, and no work reports the false-positive rate on such groups as a headline metric.

Hierarchical defenses are recent and evaluated weakly. **SHFL** (Tavallaie et al., arXiv 2024, venue unstated) has edges drop the a most distant clients and the cloud weight edges by a closed-form utility maximisation; it assumes the attacker count is known and excludes backdoors ([SHFL](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.15067v1)). **PPBR** (He et al., IEEE Transactions on Mobile Computing, February 2026) is the only work that treats edge servers as possibly Byzantine, filtering clients by trimmed k-means on DP sign statistics and edges by a median-norm band, at 36-133 MB of ciphertext per round ([PPBR](https://m64he.github.io/paper_sshen/2026/he2026ppbr.pdf)). **FoggyTrust** (arXiv 2026, preprint) pushes FLTrust to fog nodes and loses convergence speed when the root set is split ([FoggyTrust](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.27622)); **Cost-TrustFL** (arXiv 2025, preprint) multiplies regional reference cosine by an EMA reputation but gives no formula for the cloud-level trust ([Cost-TrustFL](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.20218)); **R-HFL** (Zhou et al., Electronics 2022) runs cosine detection at each edge only ([R-HFL](https://mdpi.com/2079-9292/12/1/112)); and **TWRM** (Cai et al., Future Generation Computer Systems 2024) applies subjective-logic reputation to both cluster heads and devices, though as an incentive mechanism on MNIST with its combination rule paywalled ([TWRM](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0167739X24002772)). In every work read the two levels act **in sequence on different objects**; none fuses a parent-level and a Global-level score for the same client, none lets the Global's trust in a parent discount the scores that parent issues, and all assume groups are fixed externally rather than formed from update similarity, which an attacker can influence. The empirical hierarchical assessment found the deeper hierarchy more backdoor-vulnerable ([Alqattan et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.10752v1)).

### Peer voting is published and needs an honest majority of voters

Peer validation on local data is well established. **BaFFLe** (Andreina et al., arXiv 2020, venue unconfirmed) has validators vote on the global model using per-class error and a Local Outlier Factor against accepted history, budgeting in its quorum for honest validators who judge wrongly because of skewed data, and reports 100% detection with FPR below 5% ([BaFFLe](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2011.02167)). **FedReview** (Zheng et al., arXiv 2024, venue unstated) is the closest match to per-client voting: non-training reviewers rank each update by loss on class-balanced local samples, and the server takes the median outlier count and a majority vote ([FedReview](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.16934)). FedTest (arXiv 2025, preprint) has no defense against lying testers ([FedTest](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.11167v1)); BFLC (arXiv 2020, venue unconfirmed) uses the committee median and needs over half the committee to collude ([BFLC](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2004.00773)); Biscotti (arXiv 2018) runs Multi-Krum in a stake-selected committee, tolerates 30% malicious stake and was about 14x slower than plain FL ([Biscotti](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1811.09904)); stake-and-slash voting needs a malicious voter share below one half ([Dong et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2307.00543v3)). The only schemes that exceed 50% attackers, DFL-Dual and the DMTT preprint (arXiv August 2026), work because **each client anchors trust on its own model or data rather than on a vote** ([DMTT, preprint](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.03156)). Basil's authors state that loss on local data cannot distinguish Byzantine behaviour from heterogeneity and resort to sharing 5% of raw data ([Basil](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2109.07706v3.pdf)), and DFL-Dual shows a peer can invert a neighbour's update into recognisable data, so voting raises the adversary from the server to any voter. FLCert (Cao et al., arXiv 2022, venue unconfirmed) is a different kind of vote: a majority over group models at prediction time with a tight per-input certificate, which transfers to an ensemble over parent models only if attackers cannot influence group assignment ([FLCert](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/2210.00584)).

Reputation fusion machinery is plentiful. Subjective logic combines direct and recommended opinions (Kang et al., arXiv 2019) ([Kang et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.06837)); DMTT keeps Beta evidence with forgetting factor 0.9 and an uncertainty penalty; FLARE (arXiv 2025, preprint) uses asymmetric reputation steps (+0.05 benign, −0.15 suspicious) with soft exclusion, which its ablation ranks among the most valuable components ([FLARE](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.14715v1)); HRA (arXiv 2025, preprint) multiplies a per-round geometric-median distance score by a slow reputation, reaching 98.66% versus 84.77% and 78.52% for either signal alone on a weak 10-client benchmark ([HRA](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.18044)). **None of the reputation papers excludes permanently**; exclusion is a low-reputation state that decays back, and asymmetric rates are the published counter to on-off attackers.

### Dynamic networks are mature for efficiency and untested as a defense signal

Width- and depth-scalable FL is crowded: HeteroFL (arXiv 2020, venue unconfirmed), FjORD (NeurIPS 2021), ScaleFL (Ilhan, Su, Liu, CVPR 2023; up to 2x lower inference latency and 4x smaller models at under 2% accuracy loss), DepthFL (Kim et al., ICLR 2023; 76.06% global accuracy on CIFAR-100 ResNet-18 versus 57.61% for HeteroFL), ReeFL (ICML 2024), and **X-FED** (Liu et al., CIKM 2026), which already personalizes early-exit networks and cuts inference cost by 30.79-46.86% ([ScaleFL](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/CVPR2023/html/Ilhan_ScaleFL_Resource-Adaptive_Federated_Learning_With_Heterogeneous_Clients_CVPR_2023_paper.html); [DepthFL](https://flower.ai/docs/baselines/depthfl.html); [X-FED](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.10015)). The robustness link is thin. Horus (arXiv 2025, venue unstated) states that HeteroFL-type methods have not been evaluated against poisoning and that element-wise robust aggregation is infeasible when layer shapes differ; it screens only the singular values of shared LoRA factors ([Horus](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.03579)). FedLCD (Applied Sciences, October 2026) screens per layer across clients and reports only "modest but consistent" gains over whole-model detection ([FedLCD](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-3417/16/20/10119)). No work read uses disagreement between exits or heads as a malicious-client signal; one October 2026 preprint on security-adaptive early-exit ViTs for heterogeneous FL exists by title only and must be read before claiming novelty ([arXiv 2610.24036](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.24036v1)).

## Defense cost is rarely measured and privacy rarely coexists with screening

### Overhead numbers disagree by orders of magnitude because implementation dominates

The original defense papers almost never report wall-clock time or memory. FLTrust calls its server cost "negligible"; FLAME reports overhead only as 1.67 extra rounds on average to reach 99% of undefended accuracy, and its secure two-party variant took 22,081.65 s of server time with 100 clients ([FLTrust](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2012.13995); [FLAME](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2101.02281)). The measured tables that exist come from later benchmarks and contradict each other: the SoK puts Krum and median at 15-53x the plain mean ([SoK](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.03801)), BackFed measures them at 0.33-0.44 s against 0.35 s for FedAvg on FEMNIST ([BackFed](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.04903v2)), and the SpectralKrum preprint (arXiv December 2025) times trimmed mean at 406 ms versus 7 ms for Krum ([SpectralKrum, preprint](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.11760)). The disagreement says implementation detail (sorting strategy, device, whether the n × d matrix is materialised) outweighs asymptotic class, so every baseline must be re-timed in one backend on named hardware. **No paper found reports peak server RAM for a defense**, an open measurement niche.

Cheap screening has recent support. The PDR preprint (Zuo et al., arXiv May 2026) runs distance-based aggregators on a fresh sparse random projection to k = 4096 dimensions each round and applies the resulting weights to full gradients, with a (1 + ε)/(1 − ε) penalty on the robustness error; Krum on CIFAR-100 with 30% Byzantine clients fell from 8.24 s to 0.075 s with accuracy unchanged or better, but the method "does not apply to coordinate-wise rules such as coordinate-wise median or trimmed mean" ([PDR, preprint](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.28335v1)). SketchGuard (arXiv 2025, preprint) matches full-precision screening in decentralised FL with Count Sketches of k = 350-1,000, but costs more than it saves below about 32 neighbours, so at 15-30 clients the gain is memory, not wall-clock ([SketchGuard, preprint](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.07922v2)). Restricting screening to output layers is cheaper still but evadable: backdoor-critical layers are often convolutional and look benign under Krum distance, and the LP attack bypasses MultiKrum and FLAME in all its settings ([LP attack, ICLR 2024](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2308.04466v2)).

On the training side, activations rather than parameters dominate memory, so depth-wise or progressive training (ProgFed, ICML 2022; FeDepth, AAAI 2024; NeuLite, arXiv 2024 with up to 50.4% peak-memory reduction) saves real memory where width scaling mainly saves parameters, and multi-exit DepthFL has the largest measured footprint ([FeDepth](https://arxiv.org/html/2303.04887v3); [NeuLite](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.10826v1); [PracMHBench, preprint](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.08750v1)). Compression and Byzantine robustness are provably compatible: Byz-VR-MARINA (ICLR 2023) and its AISTATS 2024 successors combine compression with robust aggregation, using coordinate median with bucketing in experiments ([Rammal et al., AISTATS 2024](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.09804)), and signSGD with majority vote (ICLR 2019) sends 32x less while tolerating up to 50% adversarial workers of the classes considered ([signSGD](https://arxiv.org/abs/1810.05291v3)). For simulation, keeping one model resident on the device and updating it in place made pfl-research 7-72x faster than other simulators ([pfl-research](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.06430v2)). Energy accounting is immature: Meta's Green FL study finds configurations of similar accuracy differing up to 200x in carbon ([Green FL, preprint](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2303.14604)), and CodeCarbon does not actually read Apple Silicon or Intel GPU power in a default install ([CodeCarbon docs](https://docs.codecarbon.io/latest/explanation/methodology/)).

### Screening needs to see updates, and the private alternatives check only simple predicates

The privacy case for hiding individual updates is real but narrower than early gradient-inversion papers suggested. Careful evaluations find reconstruction degrades sharply with larger batches, more local iterations and batch norm in training mode: without the private batch's BN statistics a single ImageNet image "fails to return a recognizable reconstruction" (Huang et al., NeurIPS 2021) ([Huang et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2112.00059)); NVIDIA's study concludes earlier work "likely exaggerated the risks" ([Hatamizadeh et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2202.06924)); and a USENIX Security 2025 SoK calls gradient inversion "notably constrained, fragile, and easily defensible" ([Du et al., USENIX Security 2025](https://www.usenix.org/conference/usenixsecurity25/presentation/du)). The counter-evidence is SME (Zhu, Yao, Blaschko, ICML 2023), which partially reconstructs CIFAR-100 images from multi-epoch weight updates when a client holds 50 or fewer images ([SME](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2306.00127)). **Membership and property inference** are the stronger reason to protect updates: participants can infer properties unrelated to the task from others' updates (Melis et al., IEEE S&P 2019), and neither local nor central DP stops property inference (Naseri, Hayes, De Cristofaro, NDSS 2022) ([Melis et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1805.04049); [Naseri et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2009.03561)). Secure aggregation is now cheap, with Flamingo (IEEE S&P 2023 per ePrint) training at about 1.4-1.7x the non-private time, but it validates nothing, declares malicious inputs out of scope, and can be eluded by a malicious server (Pasquini et al., ACM CCS 2022) ([Flamingo](https://arxiv.org/html/2308.09883v4); [Pasquini et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2111.07380v5)).

Published reconciliations fall into three families with steeply rising cost. **Single-server proofs over hidden updates** check simple predicates: RoFL (IEEE S&P 2023) and ACORN (USENIX Security 2023) verify norm bounds, but RoFL's own results show norm bounds do not stop continuous tail-target backdoors, which reach 50% accuracy after 500 rounds on CIFAR-10 ([RoFL](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/2107.03311); [ACORN](https://eprint.iacr.org/2022/1461)); EIFFeL (ACM CCS 2022) and NoV (ACM CCS 2024) check cosine similarity against the **public** previous global update, at 2.4-20.5 s per iteration for d = 1K-10K models in EIFFeL ([EIFFeL](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2112.12727); [NoV](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.01080)). **Two non-colluding servers** run richer rules in MPC: Private FLAME (USENIX Security 2022) runs cosine clustering under two-party computation, FLOD (ePrint 2021, venue unconfirmed) runs a sign-based FLTrust variant, and WW-FL (arXiv 2023) measures 315 s per LeNet iteration under MPC versus 0.07 s in plaintext ([FLOD](https://eprint.iacr.org/2021/993.pdf); [WW-FL](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2302.09904)). **Revealing a controlled statistic**: BREA (IEEE JSAC) lets the server learn pairwise distances for Multi-Krum ([BREA](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2007.11115)). Cosine to a public reference is therefore provably checkable in private; pairwise client comparison and clustering are not cheap. DP does not resolve the tension: clipping plus noise blunts backdoors (LDP and CDP at ε = 3 cut EMNIST backdoor accuracy from 88% to 10% and 6%, at 62% and 78% main accuracy versus 90% for norm bounding) ([Naseri et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2009.03561)), but DP and Byzantine resilience compose badly as dimension grows, requiring f/n ∈ O(b/(√d + b)) (Guerraoui et al., PODC 2021), a trilemma made tight by Allouah et al. (ICML 2023) ([Guerraoui et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2102.08166); [Allouah et al., ICML 2023](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v202/allouah23a.html)).

Hierarchy offers the most natural compromise. **SHARE** (Velicheti, Xia, Koyejo, arXiv 2021, with a NeurIPS 2021 workshop listing) securely aggregates inside random clusters and robustly aggregates cluster means, tolerating q0/m adversaries for cluster size m, and names cluster size as the privacy-robustness knob; reclustering erodes privacy as the server sees more averages, and heterogeneous data is left to future work ([SHARE](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2110.02940)). **FedGT** (Xhemrishi et al., arXiv 2024, venue unconfirmed) uses overlapping groups from a parity-check matrix to identify malicious clients from group aggregates alone with 15 clients in 8 groups of 4, cutting targeted-attack accuracy to 5.32% versus 25.16% for RFA, but over-flags benign heterogeneous clients on ISIC2019 ([FedGT](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.05506)). PPBR is the only work combining a fixed two-level hierarchy, within-group secure aggregation, screening at both levels and non-IID poisoning evaluation, on small MNIST-class models ([PPBR](https://m64he.github.io/paper_sshen/2026/he2026ppbr.pdf)). Peer evaluation is worse for privacy: decentralisation "does not increase privacy", and a passive neighbour can infer membership better than in federated settings (Pasquini, Raynal, Troncoso, IEEE S&P 2023) ([Pasquini et al., S&P 2023](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2205.08443)); published peer designs therefore show peers only aggregates (BaFFLe; Zhao et al.'s noisy sub-models) or confine inspection to attested enclaves (CrowdGuard) ([Zhao et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1910.13111); [CrowdGuard](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2210.07714)).

## Reviewers in 2026 expect unified protocols, adaptive attacks and per-client metrics

The critiques converge. Khan et al.'s survey of 50 top-tier defense papers ("Decoding FL Defenses", arXiv 2025, preprint, extending a 2023 workshop paper) finds about 30% evaluate only on MNIST, about 50% use IID data, about 40% use FedSGD, about 40% use only naive attacks, only 24% test cross-device settings and **only 4% report personalized per-client metrics**; with 20% malicious clients FedAvg loses under 1% on MNIST but up to 50% on CIFAR-10, and FLDetector detects perfectly on MNIST yet has a false-negative rate of 1 on FEMNIST ([Khan et al.](https://yasrachandio.github.io/assets/pdfs/Khan_FL_defense_2025.pdf)). Unified benchmarks show reported gains shrinking or vanishing: no universal winner and an accuracy cost when all clients are benign (FedSecurity, arXiv 2023, KDD 2024 per listings) ([FedSecurity](https://arxiv.org/html/2306.04959v5)); data-specific hyperparameters that do not transfer (FLPoison) ([SoK](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.03801)); and bugs in published attack code, including triggers applied after normalisation and a DeepSight distance bug in 3DFed whose fix "degrades significantly" the attack (BackFed) ([BackFed](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.04903v2)).

Accepted 2024-2026 papers set a recognisable bar. FoundationFL (NDSS 2025) uses six datasets, 100 clients, 1,000-3,000 rounds, a 0-50% attacker sweep, two adaptive attacks and an unknown-f ablation ([FoundationFL](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.17381v1)); AlignIns (CVPR 2025) uses 20 clients with full participation as its main setting but adds 100 clients with 20 sampled, a Dirichlet sweep of 0.1-1.0, ten baselines including an oracle that removes attackers perfectly, two adaptive attacks and per-round timing ([AlignIns](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.07978v2)); MARS (NeurIPS 2025) reports ACC, ASR, TPR, FPR and a composite score, a 0-95% attacker sweep, a regulariser-swept adaptive attack and 6.57 s per round against 2.07 s for FedAvg ([MARS](https://www.arxiv.org/pdf/2509.20383)); Bad-PFL (ICLR 2025) computes accuracy and ASR **on personalized models**, showing global-model ASR near 90% can differ substantially from personalized ASR ([Bad-PFL](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.12736v1)). Small vision models remain publishable: CIFAR-scale papers were accepted at NDSS 2025, CVPR 2025, ICLR 2025, NeurIPS 2025, ICLR 2026 and AAAI 2026, though accepted papers usually add a pretrained or transformer experiment, and a foundation-model branch is forming at ICML, NeurIPS and ACL ([IndexGuard, ICML 2026](https://dspace.networks.imdea.org/bitstream/handle/20.500.12761/2048/IndexGuard_ICML2026.pdf?sequence=1); [Safe-FedLLM, ACL 2026](https://aclanthology.org/2026.acl-long.1120.pdf)). An FL experimental-design recipe (Morafah, Wang, Lin, arXiv 2023, venue unconfirmed) adds that sampling rates above 0.4 "can mask the effect of statistical heterogeneity", that at Dirichlet 0.05 a local-only baseline is competitive and must be reported, and that results should average at least three runs ([Morafah et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2307.15245v1.pdf)).

Statistical reporting is weak across the field: no seed count or standard deviation appears in the text of FoundationFL, AlignIns, Bad-PFL or BackFed, and MARS states a fixed seed with single values. The methodological literature asks for more, recommending interval estimates such as stratified bootstrap confidence intervals and noting that apparent rankings can reverse once uncertainty is included (Agarwal et al., NeurIPS 2021) ([Agarwal et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2108.13264)). Taken together, the mandatory set for a detection-based personalized defense is: clean accuracy under attack; ASR, including on personalized models and after the attack window closes; a no-attack row with the defense active; TPR and FPR stated separately, with the false-positive cost on the most skewed honest clients shown explicitly; sweeps over attacker fraction and heterogeneity; personalized-FL baselines (Ditto, FedRep, FedBN) alongside robust ones; an unknown-f experiment; and server overhead measured on named hardware.

## Seven open gaps a hierarchical personalized defense paper can claim

The team's own experiments, reported here as the team's results rather than literature, sharpen where a FedHEP-based paper can contribute. FedHEP+ (built on the team's AAMAS paper FedHEP: a multi-head ResNet-9 with clients grouped under parents and a Global server) screens each client update by a robust z-score (median/MAD) of its cosine similarity to a coordinate-median reference, accumulates evidence in a moving average, and isolates offenders permanently. On CIFAR-100 at α = 0.5 with 15 clients, 3 attackers and 3 seeds, the team measured that it beats the authors' Defended FedHEP under sign flip (46.3 vs 38.1) and label flip and cuts backdoor success from 79% to 21%; that it falsely isolates honest clients mid-training as honest disagreement (MAD) grows; that Bulyan and FLAME stop backdoors (0.5%) but cost 4-6 points with no attack; and that under ALIE and Min-Max the attackers' z-scores sit far **above** the honest ones, so the one-sided screen never fires, and none of Defended FedHEP, FedHEP+, Bulyan or FLAME isolated a single adaptive attacker. Note that the FedHEP paper itself could not be found in any index; the only public artifact is the Topology-aware-FDL repository, whose README describes the same root/parent/local-head architecture but reports only MNIST and CIFAR-10 and does not name the paper ([Topology-aware-FDL](https://github.com/nam200718/Topology-aware-FDL)), so the paper should cite the team's own copy for FedHEP's claims.

### Gap 1: in-distribution attackers defeat every outlier screen, so detection must be paired with influence bounds

The team's ALIE and Min-Max result is what the attack literature predicts rather than an anomaly: ALIE sets every coordinate to μ + zσ, Min-Max binary-searches γ so the malicious update stays inside the benign distance range, and mimic has cosine 1 with an honest client ([ALIE](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1902.06156.pdf); [NDSS 2021](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10286354); [ICLR 2022](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2006.09365)). XFED scales its perturbation by the median and MAD of recent global deltas, which targets a MAD-based screen almost by construction ([XFED, preprint](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.09489)). Attackers that hide closer to consensus than honest clients do leave two exploitable signatures the literature has used separately but never in a hierarchical personalized setting: **abnormal mutual similarity among colluders** (ALIE attackers send identical vectors, which FoolsGold's sybil logic penalises, and which DarkFed had to add a loss term to suppress) ([FoolsGold](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1808.04866.pdf); [DarkFed](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.03299v1)), and **abnormal temporal consistency** (PoisonedFL's fixed sign vector, which defeats FLDetector precisely because it is consistent) ([PoisonedFL](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.15611v3)). A two-sided or under-dispersion test is the obvious extension, but the theory warns against relying on detection: within the honest spread no filter can separate attackers, and the error floor Ω((f/n)G²) remains even with perfect filtering ([AISTATS 2023](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v206/allouah23a.html); [AlignIns](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.07978v2)). The defensible design is a screen for gross attacks layered on an influence-bounding aggregator (worker momentum, NNM, trimmed mean, ARC), so that an undetected in-distribution attacker can only shift the aggregate by a bounded amount. No paper evaluates that layering on a multi-head personalized hierarchy, and no independent test of AlignIns, LASA or FoundationFL against PoisonedFL or the pill attack exists, so both are open comparisons.

### Gap 2: false isolation under growing honest dispersion is unmeasured and permanent isolation is unsupported

The team's mid-training false isolations match what the (G, B) model predicts: heterogeneity G² does not shrink as the global gradient shrinks, so honest cosine to any central reference falls as training proceeds, and a screen calibrated early produces late false positives ([NeurIPS 2023](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2309.13591)). **No paper found reports honest-client false-isolation rates as a function of training round under non-IID data**, and AlignIns and LASA report no honest false-positive rates at all, so this measurement is itself publishable. The literature offers three fixes to test: re-estimate the null distribution every round or widen the radius per removal pass (AFA); test whether a round is bimodal before removing anyone (FLDetector's gap statistic, MESAS's significance tests); and make exclusion reversible or soft, through pardon rules (FedDLAD), forgetting factors (DMTT), asymmetric reputation steps (FLARE) or personalization weights that shrink collaboration as heterogeneity rises (NeurIPS 2024) ([AFA](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1909.05125); [MESAS](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.03600v2); [FedDLAD](https://www.ijcai.org/proceedings/2025/0559.pdf); [FLARE](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.14715v1); [Allouah et al., NeurIPS 2024](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2409.20329)). Permanent isolation appears only in AFA, FLDetector and FedCAP, each with documented costs, and all three Alharbi methods let flagged clients return. Under label skew a permanently isolated honest client takes rare-class information with it, and a reversible rule must then be tested against on-off attackers ([Lewis et al., JMLR 2023](https://www.jmlr.org/papers/v24/22-0014.html)).

### Gap 3: the screening statistic, not the scale estimator, is the weak component

MAD is a sound scale estimator and stronger than the standard-deviation z-scores in AFA, AlignIns and LASA. The weak parts are what is measured. The coordinate median has the worst robustness coefficient of the standard rules and collapses under non-IID with no attack ([AISTATS 2023](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v206/allouah23a.html); [Li, Ngai, Voigt](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2302.07173)); whole-model cosine mixes intended personalization in the heads with attack signal; and cosine statistics are what the pill attack and DarkFed explicitly match ([pill](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.15389)). The evidence points to computing the reference with NNM plus trimmed mean over momentum-smoothed updates or using the previous global direction, restricting screening to the shared trunk and computing it per layer (LASA, MESAS), and adding a top-k sign-agreement statistic (AlignIns) that reports results on ResNet-9 and CIFAR-100. Because PDR's random-projection speed-ups do not apply to coordinate-wise rules, swapping the coordinate-median reference for a vector-level centre also makes cheap sketched screening possible ([PDR, preprint](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.28335v1)). Restricting screening to heads alone would be a mistake, since backdoor-critical layers are often convolutional ([LP attack](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2308.04466v2)).

### Gap 4: no backdoor defense for personalized hierarchies avoids the clean-accuracy tax

The team's comparison reproduces the field's central trade-off: FLAME and Bulyan suppress backdoors at a clean-accuracy cost (FLAME's 39.3% FPR in BackFed; Bulyan at 39.57% benign CIFAR-10 accuracy in A3FL's table), while cosine screening misses backdoors that barely move direction ([BackFed](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.04903); [A3FL](https://faculty.ist.psu.edu/wu/papers/A3FL-NeurIPS_2023.pdf)). No defense designed for hierarchical or personalized multi-head FL exists, and two top-venue attack papers say existing defenses give pFL "a false sense of security" ([PFedBA](https://www.usenix.org/conference/usenixsecurity24/presentation/lyu); [Bad-PFL](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.12736v1)). Open questions include ASR measured per head (root, parent, local, ensemble), since the local head blocks black-box backdoors but the aggregated root and parent heads reopen a shared path; resistance to BapFL-style encoder-only poisoning, PFedBA and Bad-PFL; resistance to A3FL or IBA, which no hybrid has survived in an independent benchmark; and durability after the attack stops, with recovery (FedRecover or FedEraser-style replay) once a client is isolated. Signals with the best reported non-IID behaviour (sign agreement, subspace consensus, planted indicators, per-client reconstruction) have never been tried inside a hierarchy, and a probe-set score in the SD-CSFL style would need per-parent thresholds and class-restricted statistics, since entropy on class-balanced synthetic data penalises honest clients with narrow label sets ([SD-CSFL](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/WACV2026/papers/Alharbi_SD-CSFL_A_Synthetic_Data-Driven_Conformity_Scoring_Framework_for_Robust_Federated_WACV_2026_paper.pdf)).

### Gap 5: hierarchy-native robustness is largely unclaimed

Every hierarchical defense read acts level by level on different objects, trusts its middle tier, and assumes fixed groups. Five design points therefore look open, subject to reading TWRM's paywalled mechanism and the unread arXiv 2610.24036 before claiming them: a **fused client score** combining parent-level and Global-level evidence, ablated against edge-only (R-HFL, FoggyTrust), sequential (SHFL, PPBR) and product-of-trust (Cost-TrustFL) baselines; **discounting a parent's opinions by the Global's trust in that parent**, the subjective-logic tool for a compromised recommender, which matters because a parent with an attacker majority will rank its own honest members as outliers ([Kang et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.06837)); **attacker placement as a threat model**, since similarity-formed groups can be steered (the S&P 2025 cluster-poisoning attack) and a small group can have an attacker majority when the population does not, a per-group analogue of the ICML 2024 subsampling result ([Vo et al.](https://research-repository.griffith.edu.au/items/7955ed1f-56b6-431a-9e67-5f6619495648); [ICML 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.12780v2)); **soft quarantine**, letting a suspicious group keep its parent head while being down-weighted at the root; and **head-disagreement as an anomaly signal**, using the root/parent/local heads FedHEP already has. Peer voting by itself is published (FedReview, BaFFLe, BFLC) and needs an honest voter majority, so novelty would lie in voting restricted to same-parent peers, ballots weighted by each voter's independent trust score, or votes on heads rather than full weights, and any design must state what voters receive given that peer exchange "does not increase privacy" ([FedReview](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.16934); [Pasquini et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2205.08443)).

### Gap 6: privacy and cost are unaddressed for hierarchical screening

FedHEP's structure maps naturally onto SHARE: secure aggregation inside each parent, Global screening of parent aggregates, with privacy equal to secure aggregation over the group size ([SHARE](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2110.02940)). The second stage, checking individual clients under a flagged parent, is exactly what that pattern cannot do privately; the published options are FedGT-style overlapping groups, cosine checks against a public reference under zero-knowledge proofs (EIFFeL, NoV), or revealing updates only inside flagged groups as a stated conditional cost ([FedGT](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.05506); [EIFFeL](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2112.12727)). No study covers fixed, similarity-formed groups under non-IID personalization. On cost, no defense paper reports peak server memory, overhead tables disagree by orders of magnitude, and RKD-style distillation runs at 42 s per round ([RKD](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.00587v2)), so measured memory and time on the team's own hardware would be a genuine contribution.

### Gap 7: the evaluation must clear a bar the current protocol does not

Against the bar in the previous section, the current protocol is exposed on several fronts: one dataset, 15 clients with full participation (above the 0.4 sampling rate said to mask heterogeneity), a single attacker fraction of 20% that S&P 2022 would call unrealistic without a cross-silo justification, a round budget the evaluation notes record as 30 (far below the 100-1,000 rounds of accepted CIFAR-scale papers, which leaves late-start and durability attacks untested), and no personalized-FL baselines, so reviewers can attribute gains to personalization rather than defense. Bottom-10% client accuracy, which only 4% of surveyed papers report, and the team's three seeds are strengths if paired with paired-seed bootstrap intervals ([Khan et al.](https://yasrachandio.github.io/assets/pdfs/Khan_FL_defense_2025.pdf); [Agarwal et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2108.13264)). On timing, the AAMAS 2027 main-track deadline (8 October 2026) has passed; IJCAI 2027 is reported by an aggregator, unverified, at 11 January 2027 ([AAMAS 2027 call](https://warwick.ac.uk/fac/sci/dcs/aamas2027/calls/call-for-main-track/); [OpenCurious IJCAI 2027](https://www.opencurious.com/ai-conference-deadlines/ijcai-2027)).

## Addendum (2026-11-25): honest outliers at scale, and what the screen's own logs say

The team's offline replay of FedHEP-Guard's screen (report 8.2, `scripts/replay_screen.py`) narrows the question this review has to answer. Backdoor attackers sit on the low side of the screen's scores, colluding ALIE and Min-Max attackers on the high side in rounds that split sharply (gap ratio 30–130), and honest outliers on both sides. The gap gate opens as often in backdoor rounds as in attack-free ones, and the separation of backdoor attackers from honest clients falls from AUROC 0.93 (15 clients) to 0.73 (50 clients) on a single round's scores, but stays at 0.96 and 0.81 when each client's score is smoothed over rounds. Four lines of work bear on this.

**Temporal accumulation before deciding.** FLDetector averages each client's suspicious score over a window of N rounds, normalises it per round, and only then applies its gap-statistic test of whether an attack exists, followed by 2-means clustering ([FLDetector](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2207.09209)). FLTracer runs a per-client Kalman filter over sign, ordering and classifier features (99.13% TPR, 2.29% FPR at Dirichlet 0.5, self-reported; [FLTracer](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.13424v1)). Both decide on accumulated per-client evidence rather than on one round's geometry, which is what the replay's smoothed score suggests. FedHEP-Guard already accumulates evidence, but only after a per-round gate has zeroed it; the open design is to gate on the accumulated score. The known weakness carries over: an attacker that is consistent from the first round (PoisonedFL) looks benign to a consistency test ([PoisonedFL](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.15611v3)), so accumulation must stay a population comparison, not a self-comparison.

**Grouping by inferred data distribution.** BoBa infers each client's label distribution from peaks in its last-layer update, forms overlapping per-class clusters, and has clients vote for their nearest neighbours inside each cluster, with trust accumulated over rounds; it reports backdoor success of at most 0.9% on CIFAR-10 with 50 clients and 10% attackers ([BoBa](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.09658v1)). Its own limits are relevant: distribution inference is weaker at mild skew (0.89–0.96 accuracy at its mildest setting) and the pairwise computation scales as O(n²). The team's peer reference (Phase 7, report 7.5) grouped clients by class masks and made honest isolations worse at α 0.1, so distribution-aware grouping alone is not the answer here; BoBa's voting and accumulation are the untested parts.

**Learned separation.** FLBuff models non-IID drift as an omni-directional expansion of benign representations and backdoors as a single-direction push, and trains a supervised contrastive encoder to keep a buffer between them, with nearest-neighbour trust; it reports about 1% backdoor success where baselines exceed 15–20% ([FLBuff](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.23511v1)). It needs labelled malicious examples to train the encoder, which a deployment rarely has.

**Server-side data instead of geometry.** SafeFL distils a 100-sample synthetic set from the first 25–30 global models and scores each upload by its loss on that set, reporting 0% false positives on non-IID CIFAR-10 with no attack, where it measures FLAME and FLDetector flagging about half of the benign clients ([SafeFL, preprint](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.09110v1)). This is the family of the team's SD-CSFL line (synthetic-data conformity scoring). In FedHEP-Guard's Phase 6 a probe-logits signal caught no backdoor attacker at D1 (report 7.4), so an output-space score needs its own test before it is relied on.

**What follows for FedHEP-Guard.** (1) Treat the two sides of the screen differently: the low side for backdoors at the normal gate, the high side only in sharply split rounds; this is under test on the Acer (`plus.high_gate`). (2) Build the next signal from accumulated per-client scores, gated after accumulation. (3) Report honest-client false-isolation rates by round and by number of clients; independent benchmarks find attack-free false-positive rates of 25–39% for FLAME and DeepSight ([BackFed](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.04903)), and no paper found reports them as a function of client count.

## Conclusion

The two failures in the team's own runs (honest clients isolated as their disagreement grows, adaptive attackers never isolated because they sit too close to the consensus) come from one assumption: that distance from consensus indicates malice. Non-IID training breaks that assumption in one direction and in-distribution attacks break it in the other. The literature's most durable results have moved away from it, either by bounding what any client can do (momentum, NNM, trimmed mean, ARC) or by anchoring trust outside cross-client geometry (a trusted reference, a client's own history, reconstruction, a planted task). A FedHEP-based paper is strongest if it treats consensus distance as one signal among several (temporal consistency, colluder similarity, cross-head disagreement), applies soft and reversible weights instead of permanent verdicts, and places all of it on top of an influence-bounding aggregator, so that the attacks it cannot see still cause only bounded harm.

The hierarchy cuts both ways. It brings risks flat FL lacks: per-group attacker majorities, similarity-based grouping that attackers can steer, and parents that can vouch for the wrong members. It also offers what flat FL cannot: a natural secure-aggregation boundary, two independent vantage points on every client, and an ensemble of heads whose disagreement costs nothing extra to compute. Given how widely self-reported and independently benchmarked results diverge in this field, a candid paper that measures false isolation over time, reports that no tested defense caught ALIE or Min-Max, and then shows a hierarchy-native mechanism closing part of that gap under a protocol that meets current standards would be more valuable than another defense that wins only in its own setting.
